Case Overview

Understanding the Dispute

A dual-treaty case addressing terrorism financing in eastern Ukraine and racial discrimination in Crimea.

Case Summary

On January 31, 2024, the International Court of Justice issued its merits judgment in this landmark case. Ukraine alleged that the Russian Federation violated two international conventions: the ICSFT (International Convention for the Suppression of the Financing of Terrorism), in connection with terrorism financing in eastern Ukraine, and the CERD (International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination), concerning racial discrimination against Crimean Tatars and ethnic Ukrainians in Crimea.

The Court found Russia violated ICSFT Article 9, paragraph 1 (obligation to investigate), and CERD Articles 2(1)(a) and 5(e)(v) (racial discrimination in education). The Court also found Russia violated two of three provisional measures ordered in April 2017. No compensation was ordered.

The case represents one of the most comprehensive treaty-based disputes examined by the Court, spanning both counter-terrorism and anti-discrimination law.

Central Legal Questions

Did Russia fail its obligations under ICSFT to investigate terrorism financing? Did Russia discriminate against Crimean Tatars and ethnic Ukrainians in Crimea on the basis of ethnic origin?

Judgment Breakdown

Detailed analysis of the Court's January 2024 merits judgment across ICSFT, CERD, and provisional measures.

Judgment Summary

The Court examined 16 findings across three categories: ICSFT obligations, CERD obligations, and compliance with the 2017 provisional measures order. Four violations were found, with the majority of Ukraine's claims not established.

4 Violations Found
11 Not Established
1 Partial
16 Total Findings

ICSFT Findings

1 Art. 18 — Cooperation in Prevention
Rejected

Ukraine claimed Russia failed to prevent terrorism financing. The Court held that Article 18 creates an obligation to "cooperate in prevention," not an obligation to "prevent." Evidence of border policing was limited to weapons transfers, which fall outside the scope of the ICSFT.

2 Art. 8 — Freezing of Funds
Rejected

The obligation to freeze funds arises only when there are reasonable grounds to believe funds are connected to terrorism financing. Ukraine's requests to Russia contained only "vague and highly generalized descriptions" of alleged offences, insufficient to trigger the obligation.

"The requests contained only vague and highly generalized descriptions." — paras. 97–98
3 Art. 9(1) — Investigation
Violation Found

Russia failed to even identify alleged offenders almost one year after receiving allegations from Ukraine. The Court concluded this constituted a clear violation of Russia's obligation to investigate under the ICSFT.

"The Court concludes that the Russian Federation has violated its obligations under Article 9, paragraph 1." — para. 111
4 Art. 10 — Prosecution or Extradition
Rejected

The information provided to Russia did not give rise to reasonable grounds for prosecution. Consequently, the aut dedere aut judicare obligation under Article 10 was not triggered.

"The information did not give rise to reasonable grounds." — paras. 119–120
5 Art. 12 — Mutual Legal Assistance
Rejected

Of Ukraine's 12+ requests for mutual legal assistance, only 3 fell within the scope of the ICSFT. Those 3 requests did not describe the predicate acts in sufficient detail to trigger Russia's obligation to provide assistance.

CERD Findings

1 Violence — Disappearances, Murders, Torture
Rejected

The Court found that violence including disappearances, murders, abductions, and torture was also suffered by persons of Russian and Central Asian origin. The Court was not convinced that the violence was based on ethnic origin.

2 Law Enforcement — Searches, Detentions
Partial

UN reports found that "Crimean Tatars [were] disproportionately subjected to police and FSB raids." The Court found a disparate effect on Crimean Tatars but attributed the measures to their political opposition activities rather than their ethnic origin.

3 Ban on the Mejlis
Rejected

The Court found the Mejlis was banned due to its political activities, not the ethnic origin of its members. The Qurultay (the broader Crimean Tatar representative assembly) was not banned, which undermined the argument of ethnic targeting.

4 Citizenship — Automatic Imposition
Rejected

The consequences of citizenship status flowed from the citizen/foreigner distinction regardless of ethnicity. The Court was not persuaded that the citizenship measures were based on ethnic origin.

5 Gatherings — Restrictions on Assemblies
Rejected

Restrictions on events such as Ukrainian Flag Day and Shevchenko birthday commemorations had a disparate effect on ethnic Ukrainians, but Russia provided non-ethnic explanations that the Court accepted.

6 Media — Restrictions on Ukrainian Media
Rejected

The Court found no convincing evidence that media restrictions were based on the ethnic origin of the affected communities.

7 Education — Ukrainian-Language Instruction
Violation Found

Russia failed to explain the "sudden and radical changes" in Ukrainian-language instruction in Crimea. The Court emphasized the deep connection between language and ethnic identity in finding this constituted racial discrimination under CERD.

"Language is often an essential social bond among the members of an ethnic group." — paras. 355, 370

Provisional Measures Compliance (Order of 19 April 2017)

1 Mejlis Ban — Ensure Availability of Representative Institutions
Violation

Russia maintained the ban on the Mejlis despite the Court's provisional measures order requiring it to ensure the availability of Crimean Tatar representative institutions.

"Independent of the conclusion that the ban does not violate CERD, Russia violated the provisional measures order by maintaining it." — para. 392
2 Ukrainian-Language Education — Ensure Availability
Not Violated

Despite the merits finding of a CERD violation regarding education, the Court found that Ukrainian-language education remained "available" in Crimea during the provisional measures period, with one school and 13 classes serving 318 children.

"Ukrainian-language education remained available." — para. 395
3 Non-Aggravation of the Dispute
Violation

Russia recognized the DPR and LPR and launched the "special military operation" in February 2022, severely aggravating the dispute.

"Severely undermined the basis for mutual trust." — paras. 397–398

Legal Context: ICSFT and CERD

ICSFT Article 2 — Terrorism Financing Offence

"Any person commits an offence within the meaning of this Convention if that person by any means, directly or indirectly, unlawfully and wilfully, provides or collects funds with the intention that they should be used or in the knowledge that they are to be used, in full or in part, in order to carry out [terrorist acts]."

CERD Article 1(1) — Racial Discrimination

"In this Convention, the term 'racial discrimination' shall mean any distinction, exclusion, restriction or preference based on race, colour, descent, or national or ethnic origin which has the purpose or effect of nullifying or impairing the recognition, enjoyment or exercise, on an equal footing, of human rights and fundamental freedoms in the political, economic, social, cultural or any other field of public life."

The ICSFT (1999) requires States parties to criminalize terrorism financing and to cooperate in its prevention and investigation. The Court's jurisdiction was based on Article 24(1) of the Convention, which provides for ICJ dispute resolution.

The CERD (1965) obligates States parties to eliminate racial discrimination in all its forms. Article 22 provides for ICJ jurisdiction over disputes about the Convention's interpretation or application. The Court had to determine whether Russia's measures in Crimea were based on ethnic origin or on other, non-prohibited grounds.

A key challenge in the CERD analysis was distinguishing between measures targeting ethnic groups and measures targeting political opposition that happened to disproportionately affect certain ethnic communities. The Court largely accepted Russia's non-ethnic explanations, except regarding education.
