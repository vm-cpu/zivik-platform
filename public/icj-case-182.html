Case Overview

Understanding the Dispute

A landmark case challenging Russia's justification for military intervention based on alleged genocide in eastern Ukraine.

Case Summary

On 26 February 2022, just two days after Russia's full-scale invasion began, Ukraine filed an application at the ICJ challenging Russia's claim that Ukraine committed genocide against Russian-speaking populations in the Donetsk and Luhansk oblasts.

Ukraine argues that Russia falsely accused it of genocide to justify its military aggression, and seeks a declaration from the Court that no genocide occurred and that Russia's actions based on these false claims violate international law.

The case represents a novel use of the Genocide Convention—Ukraine is essentially asking the Court to declare its innocence of genocide allegations used as a pretext for war.

Central Legal Question

Can a State invoke the ICJ's jurisdiction under the Genocide Convention to obtain a declaration that it has not committed genocide, when accused by another State?

Judgment Summary

The Court examined six preliminary objections raised by Russia, ultimately finding jurisdiction to hear Ukraine's core claim while dismissing certain aspects related to Russia's conduct.

5 Objections Rejected
1 Objection Upheld
16 Judges Participating

Preliminary Objections

1 Existence of Dispute
Rejected (15-1)

Russia argued no genuine legal dispute existed between the parties. The Court identified a "two-element argument" by Ukraine with "distinct essential characteristics that had to be examined separately."

First aspect (jurisdiction accepted): Ukraine's request to have the Court declare that Ukraine "has not committed genocide." By such a request, Ukraine does not seek to invoke Russia's international responsibility — it seeks a judicial finding that it has itself not committed the wrongful acts imputed to it in public statements.

Second aspect (jurisdiction declined): Ukraine's request to have the Court adjudicate that "the Russian Federation has acted unlawfully with respect to the Genocide Convention." This is fundamentally different — Ukraine seeks to invoke Russia's international responsibility by imputing internationally wrongful conduct to it.

"Ukraine contends, in essence, that the Russian Federation has made false allegations that [Ukraine] committed genocide in the Luhansk and Donetsk oblasts, and that the [Russian Federation] cannot lawfully, on the basis of such allegations, take any action against Ukraine under the Genocide Convention, in particular the recognition of the independence of the DPR and LPR and the launch of the 'special military operation.'" — para. 38
2 Jurisdiction Ratione Materiae
Upheld (12-4)

The Court found that Ukraine's claims regarding Russia's recognition of separatist entities and military actions fall outside the scope of the Genocide Convention. Even if Russia invoked the Convention in bad faith, this alone does not constitute a breach of Articles I and IV.

"Good faith is not itself a source of obligation where none would otherwise exist."
3 Transformation of Dispute
Rejected (15-1)

Russia argued that Ukraine's amended submissions transformed the original dispute. The Court disagreed, finding that Ukraine's amended claim merely clarified the original submission without changing the fundamental nature of the dispute.

4 No Practical Effect
Rejected (14-2)

Russia claimed a declaratory judgment would have no practical effect. The Court determined that such a judgment would clarify the parties' rights under Article I of the Genocide Convention and settle the legal dispute between them.

5 Negative Declaration Inadmissible
Rejected (13-3)

Russia argued that requests for declarations of non-violation are categorically inadmissible. The Court rejected this, establishing an important precedent that States may seek such declarations under Article IX.

"Article IX does not preclude a State seeking a declaration of non-responsibility for genocide."
6 Abuse of Process
Rejected (15-1)

Russia claimed Ukraine's application was an abuse of process. The Court found no exceptional circumstances warranting rejection on this basis, emphasizing that access to the Court should not be unduly restricted.

Two Aspects of the Dispute

The Court identified and addressed two distinct aspects of Ukraine's claims, reaching different conclusions on each:

First Aspect: Non-Responsibility Claim

Ukraine's request for a declaration that it has not committed genocide in the Donetsk and Luhansk oblasts. The Court found this falls squarely within Article IX's jurisdictional clause covering disputes about "the responsibility of a State for genocide."

Proceeds to Merits
Second Aspect: Russia's Conduct

Ukraine's claims that Russia violated international law by recognizing the DPR/LPR and launching military operations based on false genocide allegations. The Court found these claims fall outside the Genocide Convention's scope.

Legal Context: The Genocide Convention

Article IX — Dispute Resolution

"Disputes between the Contracting Parties relating to the interpretation, application or fulfilment of the present Convention, including those relating to the responsibility of a State for genocide or for any of the other acts enumerated in article III, shall be submitted to the International Court of Justice at the request of any of the parties to the dispute."

The 1948 Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide was the first human rights treaty adopted by the United Nations General Assembly. Article IX provides the ICJ with jurisdiction over disputes concerning the Convention.

This case is notable because Ukraine is using Article IX in a defensive manner—seeking a declaration that it has not committed genocide—rather than accusing another State of committing genocide. The Court's February 2024 judgment confirmed this novel use of the Convention is permissible.

Both Ukraine and Russia are parties to the Genocide Convention, which provides the legal basis for the Court's jurisdiction. Russia's preliminary objections challenged whether the dispute truly fell within the Convention's scope, particularly regarding Ukraine's claims about Russia's military actions.
